# Two adjacent minus-two pairs in an ordinary derivative

Status: proposed local structural reduction and exact necessary-model probe;
independent mathematical and row audits are pending.

Let p on13 variables take values in{-1,0,1}, with p(0)=1, every unit
value zero, and all ordinary quadratic coefficients zero or one. In its
scale16 even Fourier representation, let the constant be c in{1,2},
all quartic numerators be zero or one, exactly two distinct quadratic
numerators be minus two, and all remaining quadratic numerators be
minus one or zero. Assume the two exceptional pairs meet in a vertex.

Pair inversion forces each exceptional pair to have ordinary coefficient
one and a six-edge even top link. Every triangle contains a baseline
pair, so each present triangle belongs to exactly two tetrahedra. The
exceptional pair links are C6 or two C3s. Each exceptional edge has a
private endpoint. At that endpoint the accepted one-six-edge splitting
argument applies using only incident valences: splitting two C3s gives
Euler characteristic three, two degree-three vertices and other degrees
four or five. A sphere containing both split copies needs at least eight
vertices, and the accompanying projective plane six, exceeding thirteen.
Thus both exceptional edge links are C6.

All vertex links are now unions of closed surfaces of total characteristic
two. At either private endpoint the one-six-edge argument excludes a
seven-vertex projective-plane component, so the link is a sphere. At the
common endpoint there are two degree-six vertices and all others have
degree four or five. Every component still has positive characteristic.
If disconnected, the link consists of two projective planes. Splitting
the degree-six vertices between the two components invokes the previous
one-six-edge impossibility. Putting both in the same component needs
at least seven vertices, while the other component needs six, exceeding
the twelve available vertices. The common endpoint link is a sphere too.

At an ordinary vertex a disconnected link would be two six-vertex
projective planes on sets A,B. This forces42 skeleton edges, leaving at
most nine cross edges because E=49+c<=51. All good vertices have a cross
neighbor by the usual six-vertex-link obstruction. Every exceptional
endpoint also has a cross neighbor: its skeleton degree is at least
seven, or eight at the common endpoint, while the side plus its center
provides only six neighbors. In particular the common exceptional vertex
needs at least two cross neighbors. If every good vertex had cross degree at
least two, the nine good, two private, and one common exceptional vertex
give cross-degree sum at least22, impossible. A good cross-degree-one
vertex forces its cross neighbor to be universal to the opposite six-set
by the accepted seven-vertex link classification. Those six edges plus
one edge from every other vertex on the universal neighbor's side give
at least eleven cross edges, again impossible.

Consequently all links are spheres. Root at the common exceptional
endpoint. Its link has exactly two degree-six vertices and all other
degrees four or five. The previously independently complete inverse-
contraction sphere catalogs through order12 contain nine such types.
The two private endpoint links have exactly one degree-six vertex; their
orders are9,10,11 by the separately accepted three-type classification.
Ordinary vertex links are among the accepted flag spheres of valences
four and five. These are local flag constraints, not global flagness.

The proposed necessary models use face indicators, downward closure,
two tetrahedra per triangle, valences six at the two exceptional edges
and four/five elsewhere, exact f-vector and vertex-link identities, the
fixed root link, and every induced Euler bound0<=chi<=2. The pilot uses
integer interval propagation only; surviving rows are not functions.
Any found contradiction still needs an independent literal-row rebuild
and mathematical coverage audit before this local theorem is promoted.
